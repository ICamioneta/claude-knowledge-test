# Preface

The Commune of the Two Peoples was an egalitarian, anarchic commune that existed from the collapse of the United Terlaye in 1604 up until its integration into the Reimig Empire in 3239. Having existed for a millennium and a half, one might conclude that its government was strong, stable, and knowledgeable until the bitter end. The reality is that the commune had no government.

It takes a particular kind of culture to cultivate a stable society in where development is handled by the initiative of the commoner. A good sense of community is required to properly find and punish criminals without a centralised police force. The Commune of the Two Peoples stood as a constant foil to the rest of the organised world, not only proving that the medieval serfdoms were not a required way of life but also that development and growth was equally abundant without them, Especially in its later years, it radicalised people living under an oppressive imperial regime, showing how life would be like if you didn’t have to work for a ruler.

But, as utopian as the commune was, it was weak. Initially, it was among the largest tracts of land united under a single name. But quickly, without the people’s willingness to fight and without any organised government to give it a military, the commune’s lands were taken by more ambitious powers. For the majority of its life, the Commune of the Two Peoples remained as a community in Sefare Eysenre’s Handle, kingdoms to the west and dynasties to the east.

Although life was enjoyed under the communal existence, there was a faction of peoples who called themselves the Formists. They supported the forming of a government that could fight back against the ambitions of other nations. They believed that government could exist without power being in the hands of the few. Many ideas were formed, but the most popular and influential of these was Councilism. Councilism was the child idea of Rosefie Cetyevliefen, and her explanation of councilism was published in 3124. Forming a government remained unpopular among the majority, and so the Commune of the Two Peoples became a region in the Reimig’s expanding empire.

# Preamble

Since the beginning of our communal living, our way of life has always been under threat from external and ambitious forces. While we are armed, a mix of peasants and want-to-be soldiers does not make an army or a force capable of repelling the organised levies of a king, or the knights of a dynasty. Being against central governance has been our cause from the beginning, opposing the idea that a few should rule the many. But, there are ways that the many can rule themselves. By the usage of a pure and representative elective system, Councilism is possible.

Although proposed as a means of governance, the primary interest in councilism comes from the way it allows the military to organise itself, from the bottom to the top. Allowing the people to choose who is most competent to lead them into battle and allowing them to change their minds.

# Chapter 1: Electives

The idea of an elective is not strange to those in regular societies. When a small group of people must choose between two or a few choices, they often cast votes. A vote is a show of support for the chosen option. Whichever choice receives the most votes is put into effect. Electives are not a simple system; there is much complexity in them. Examine the simple case where a good number of people hold an elective for more than two options. Does each person cast a single vote, or do they cast votes for each option they are satisfied with? Or, perhaps, each person lists their options by preferability, and a neutral third party calculates the common order of preferability through one of several possible means?

Each system of elective suits a certain range of population sizes. Which system of elective to choose also matters depending on the options of choice available. The first case to study is when any number of people must choose between two options. First, the nature of these two options must be studied. If the two options are to perform an action or not to perform that action, this can be called a single option, with the lack of a vote representing the support for that action to be done. Depending on the nature of this action, a threshold may be used that is different to the standard 50% majority. The threshold could be increased to higher percentages such as 70% and 80% (arbitrary values) to demonstrate a more united council.

For votes where there are three or more options, how a voter casts their vote matters more. If it’s a small group of people, then vote counting isn’t plausible as votes may be cast as hands in the air. In these situations, having each person able to cast a single vote may seem like the simplest option, but consider the following scenario. An election where any number of people must choose between four options. Three of these four options are of a similar type, but each option appeals in a slightly different way. Those who vote for one of the three similar options are likely to prefer any one of the two other similar options over the dissimilar option. During the elective, the unique option gets 35% support, and the three similar options get 20%, 20%, and 25% support each. If the system enacts the most popular option without the implementation of thresholding, the unique option is enacted, even though 65% of the electorate explicitly did not want that option.

To solve this issue, the nature of the options must be understood. In a situation where it is possible for the voters to break away and enact their own options independently, say if it were a book club and the options were books of two different genres, then it may be a good idea to split the book club between those who want to assess their chosen book, or to cover the most popular book now and vow to cover the second most popular later. But, if applied to every scenario, this may create a divided organisation.

If the elective is small, where votes are cast using a hands-up system, it may be preferable to not have everyone cast a single vote but vote for every option they find merely passable. This is still not a truly representative system, because those too stubborn to settle for any option other than their first may still split the vote. There is a much higher likelihood that the option picked under this system would placate the majority of people.

For high numbers of people, a similar system exists where the populace choose whatever options they find passable, so the most passable option is chosen. This system will not do well if its options are required to be productive.

For higher numbers of people, or if the elective held is more organised, a system where the voters order the options by preferability would be a good representative system. During this system, the popularity of first choices is counted as normal. Then, the least likely option’s votes are given to the voter’s second option. There are two ways to do this: one where each vote is read again and individually sorted among their second choices, or the most popular second choice for that option receives all the votes from the least popular choice.

Which system of elective is recommended for each council is specified when required. Hands-up voting is divided between hands-up-single-vote (HUSV) and hands-up-passable-vote (HUPV). The second kind of voting for the larger population is called ballot voting, as the vote is usually cast by placing a piece of paper into a tamper-proof box. Ballot voting is divided into ballot threshold (BaT), ballot passable (BaP), ballot individually transferred (BaIT), and ballot popularly transferred (BaPT).

# Chapter 2: Councils

Whenever a community such as a town wishes to choose between several options of things to do, holding electives including every single person is not feasible. Many people will not be able to attend because they may be preoccupied. Forcing an entire town to vote on every single thing would halt the industry of a town for the time of the elective.

A Council is a small group of elected officials that act on behalf of a portion of the population they represent. The core of this idea is that there is only a few of ideas that people share; a couple of beliefs that can be had on a subject. A person from among those people can bring themselves forwards as a candidate and show their beliefs, so that people who share those beliefs can vote for them to be on the council. Council’s primary flaw compared to direct voting is that thought is diverse, and not every mix of thoughts can be represented by those few chosen to be on the council. The best way to combat this flaw is to increase the size of the council, but there is a balance to be had as a large council will more frequently have disagreements.

Councils are the primary focus of Councilism. Councilism, as a method of government, seeks to establish councils at all levels of society. Councils as a concept are already common in the workplace, workers deciding from among themselves who organises the movement of produce. Councils to manage towns, cities, regions, and the nation as a whole.
