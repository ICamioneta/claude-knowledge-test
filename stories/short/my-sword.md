A Story of Stealing

I woke up on my bed. Filled thick with straw and fibres, linen bedsheets comfortable as always. Light escapes my curtains, lighting up the room well enough to read. I get dressed, picking out my woollen work clothes from my wardrobe. I head downstairs, make myself some food, and go through the door to my shop.

I work as a carpenter. Littering the floor are my hand-crafted chairs and stools. Strown out on my shop’s shelves lay frames for paintings and tools that I worked with the blacksmith to make. Hung up on the wall is an intricate sword, floral patterns growing across the perfect blade. Cross-guard out of the finest wood and handle tied with the strongest of strings. On its pommel lay the most beautiful design, an emblem inlay on its perfectly round surface.

I opened the shop, and a ragged person walks in.

“Hi, can I help you?” I ask.

“Just looking, if that’s alright.” They said.

The person walks around for a bit.

“Anything you’re after?” I ask.

He stays silent, but his gaze falls upon the sword hanging from the wall.

“Where did you get this sword?” they ask.

“It’s an old family heirloom, given to me by my father who received it from his father before him.”

“And before that?”

“I’d like to think that his father gave it to him!” I respond.

They pulled out a piece of paper from their pocket and unfold it. They look at the paper, then at the pommel of the sword, and back at the paper. They turn to me.

“May I ask your name?” they say, politely.

“Hanver Rebbish, finest carpenter in Detorasen.”

“Rebbish?” they pull out another piece of paper, read it for a moment, then put it away again.

“What’s this symbol on the pommel?” they ask.
 
 “I’m not sure, it might have been the signature of the person in my family who had the sword 
 made. Maybe it means something in another language.”

They nod their head slightly. They look back at the sword, at the pommel, at the symbols, at the paper in their hand.

“This is my sword.” They said.

“I don’t think so, “ I responded.

“You’ve been lying. You know where this sword came from, and what the symbol is.” They accused.

“This sword belongs to my family.” They said.

“I don’t know what you’re talking about” I responded.

“I think you do. Does the name Hafacus remind you, or did your family forget to teach you their history? We’re both adults, let’s have a conversation.”

“I remember stories about the Hafacus. That they didn’t wash, that they expelled their own family members, and that they fought like sparrows.”

“See. You know.”

“So, who are you?” I asked.

“Nejer Hafacus. Descendant of Theord Hafacus, descendant of Diane Hafacus. The Hafacus family that was stolen from generation after generation by a family of thieves. The Hafacus family that lived an honest life in Leuhb until they didn’t, and their house was gone, and their farm was gone. The Hafacus family that now lives in the backstreets scraping together what they can to survive. The Hafacus family that’s lost too many children to starvation!”

Silence.

“That’s quite the legacy.”

“Legacy? You’re a family of thieves.”

“I know my father was a thief, and his father before him set him on that path. But I knew better, I knew that stealing is wrong. I abandoned everything I had to escape that, and I went to a college. I learnt the artisan’s trade, and now I live an honest life of hard work and fair prices.”

Nejer sat in thought for a short while.

“You know, our families started off on the same ground. Farmers, owners of land who tend the fields themselves and sell their grain to the City of Leuhb. They lived the same lives, hard and honest work. But neither could afford anything more. We lived from subsistence, and we both wanted to live our lives a little better. If either family could have afforded it, they would have had a child picked up and thrown into a college somewhere so that they could live better lives.”

“My family sought their lives of luxury, and they got there. I respect them for that. All they did was steal from those who didn’t deserve it.”

“Why didn’t we deserve it? Because we fought best we could and failed? Because we respect our integrity, and exiled a murderer in our own family? Can I remind you of the Rebbish that did so little as reporting his brother to the guards for burning a Hafacus house to the ground? And then what, we didn’t deserve the little left we had because you stole our bathtubs?”

“I’ve told you already, we’re not like that anymore. I’m here, living the most honest life I can. We stopped the cycle. My brothers and sisters took after me too, living honestly as craftspeople and merchants.”

“Do you think you’d be here if your grandfather didn’t steal our house? If he didn’t take our wealth? My brothers and sisters are out there dying, starving because they have nothing to their name, nothing! Not a pace of land, not a single chair.”

“What do you want me to do about it? Am I responsible for the harm done by my parents? I didn’t take anything from you, and I said I’m sorry, and I wish for your lives to improve.”

Silence.

“All I ask, all we ask, is that you take some responsibility. Accountability. Not for the people your grandfather killed, or even the people your father killed. For the people, my siblings, who die and get swept up in Leuhb because of the consequences of your parents’ actions. Actions that you benefit from.”

“That seems a little unreas…”

“I ask that you, and your siblings, apologise for the awful things you say about us. For the way you still treat us! I ask that you help us live better lives. Fund at least the basic levels of our learning, so that we can stop being the urchins of the street and have a chance at life.”

“I don’t think I can do….”

“And I want that sword.” they said, pointing at the sword on the wall.

“That sword?” I asked.

“That sword.” they said.

I looked at the sword. I looked at them. Standing, with such a strong stance. Eyes almost tearing up, desperate, but strong.

“I…” I muttered. I thought.

Silence.

“Maybe you’re right.”

“I am.” They interjected.

“The least I can do right now is give you some place to stay for the night.”

“That’s it?”

“Hey, I’ll figure something out in the morning. I’ll try to contact my siblings, see what we can pool together.”

“I understand this is a difficult situation for you, but you need to do the right thing. Show empathy. Your livelihood depends on us suffering.”

“I’m still not sure what I can do…”

“-What you will do.” They interjected.

“But you can have the sword.”

### Afterword
This is one of my prouder stories, but it is still with its flaws. I’d like to address them here, so that I can improve this dialogue and later dialogue I write.

First, the dialogue has a clear political aim, but it is extremely one-sided. Hanver doesn’t have many good points, and his world view is promoted by ignorance. When Nejer presents the information to him, he accepts it and realises that there are things to be done.

To address this, I need Hanver to have an equal emotional reaction to this. I can do this by highlighting his attempts to distance himself from his family tradition of stealing. He could reject the idea that his own success predicates on forcing others into poverty. He could make a greater effort to defend his family’s actions.

After this, I could highlight some common ground between Nejer and Hanver. Perhaps one of Hanver’s personal sacrifices to escape from his family, while Nejer remembers the sacrifices of his family to stay alive. These sacrifices aren’t equal and Nejer’s suffering is explicitly worse, this could be a point of contention where Nejer purposefully shares empathy in order to demonstrate how more difficult his own life is.
